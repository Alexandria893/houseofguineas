Setting up a guinea pig cage seems straightforward — buy a cage, add bedding, done. But the reality is that most new guinea pig owners end up with a setup that's too small, poorly laid out, or missing essentials that directly affect their pig's health. As an [exotic veterinary assistant](/san-francisco-california-exotic-veterinary-experience) in San Francisco, I've seen how much of a difference proper housing makes. Here's how to do it right from the start.

<!--more-->

## Why Cage Size Matters

This is the number one mistake new guinea pig owners make: buying a pet store cage that's far too small. Most cages marketed for guinea pigs at big box pet stores are designed for hamsters or single animals — they don't come close to meeting the space requirements for guinea pigs.

The minimum recommended cage size for two guinea pigs is **10.5 square feet** — that's a 2x4 C&C grid cage. For context, the largest cage you'll find at most pet stores is around 4-5 square feet. Less than half of what your pigs actually need.

Why does space matter so much? Guinea pigs are active, social animals. They need room to run laps (they get the zoomies just like dogs), popcorn when they're happy, and maintain separate areas for eating, sleeping, and going to the bathroom. In a cramped cage, guinea pigs become sedentary, territorial, and more prone to obesity, bumblefoot, and behavioral problems. More space genuinely means healthier, happier pigs.

## C&C Cages: The Gold Standard

A C&C cage is made from two components: **cube grids** (the wire storage cube panels you can find at most home goods stores) and a **coroplast base** (corrugated plastic sheeting). You connect the grids to form the walls and cut the coroplast to create a waterproof tray that sits inside.

Nearly every guinea pig rescue organization recommends C&C cages, and for good reason — they're affordable, customizable, and easy to build to the correct size. You can make them as large as your space allows and modify the layout as your herd grows.

**Standard C&C cage sizes:**

- **2x3 grids (7.5 sq ft)** — Absolute minimum for 1-2 guinea pigs. Workable but tight.
- **2x4 grids (10.5 sq ft)** — Recommended for 2 guinea pigs. This is the sweet spot.
- **2x5 grids (13 sq ft)** — Ideal for 3-4 guinea pigs or if you want to give a pair extra room.

## What Goes Inside Your Cage

### Bedding — The Most Important Layer

The bedding your guinea pig stands on 24 hours a day, 7 days a week directly affects their respiratory health, foot health, and comfort. This is not an area to cut corners on.

**Disposable bedding** (paper or wood shavings) is what most people start with. It's available everywhere, but it gets dusty, holds moisture against your pig's skin, and creates a recurring monthly cost of $20-40 that adds up fast. Cedar and pine shavings are especially dangerous — the phenols they release cause respiratory damage and liver issues over time.

**Basic fleece liners** are a step up, but most require a separate waterproof base, absorbent pads underneath, and 3-5 prep washes before the fleece even starts wicking. That means multiple purchases before you have a functional setup.

### Essential Accessories

Every guinea pig cage needs these basics:

- **Hides** — At least one per guinea pig, plus one extra. Guinea pigs are prey animals and need enclosed spaces where they feel safe. Without hides, they live in a constant state of low-level stress.
- **Water bottle** — Use a bottle, not a bowl. Bowls get filled with bedding, hay, and droppings within minutes. Mount the bottle on the cage grid at a comfortable drinking height.
- **Food bowl** — A heavy ceramic bowl that your pig can't flip over. They will try.
- **Hay rack or hay pile area** — Guinea pigs need unlimited timothy hay available at all times. A hay rack keeps it off the bedding, or you can designate a corner as the hay area.
- **Tunnel or bridge** — A simple cardboard tunnel or wooden bridge gives your pigs something to explore, hide in, and run through. Enrichment matters.

### Nice-to-Have Extras

Once you have the essentials covered, these additions make life better for your pigs:

- **Kitchen area** — A separate feeding zone with an easy-to-clean surface (a small piece of vinyl or an extra fleece pad). Keeps the main bedding cleaner longer.
- **Loft or second level** — If your cage layout allows it, a loft with a low-angle ramp adds usable space and gives your pigs something to explore. Make sure the ramp has traction — bare coroplast is too slippery.
- **Fleece forest** — Strips of fleece hanging from the cage grids that your guinea pigs can walk through. They love it, and it's almost free to make from scrap fleece.
- **Foraging toys** — Paper bags stuffed with hay, toilet paper rolls with treats inside, or snuffle mats. Anything that encourages natural foraging behavior.

## Cage Placement

Where you put your cage matters almost as much as what's in it.

- **Avoid direct sunlight and drafts.** Guinea pigs overheat easily and are sensitive to temperature swings. Keep the cage out of sunny windows and away from air vents or exterior doors.
- **Maintain room temperature between 65-75°F.** Guinea pigs cannot sweat and are highly vulnerable to heat stroke above 80°F. If your home runs warm, this is a serious consideration.
- **Place the cage in a social area.** Guinea pigs are social and do best in rooms where the family spends time — a living room or home office works well. Isolation in a back bedroom leads to depression and less human bonding.
- **Elevate the cage if you have other pets.** If you have cats or dogs, placing the cage on a table or sturdy stand reduces stress and keeps your pigs at a safe distance from curious noses.

## Common Cage Mistakes

I see these over and over, and they're all avoidable:

- **Cage too small.** If you bought it at a pet store and it says "guinea pig" on the box, it's almost certainly too small. Measure your space and build or buy a proper C&C cage.
- **Wire-bottom floors.** Wire flooring causes bumblefoot — painful bacterial infections of the foot pads. Guinea pigs need a solid, flat surface.
- **Cedar or pine bedding.** The phenols in these wood shavings are toxic to guinea pigs. If you use wood shavings at all, only use kiln-dried aspen — but fleece is still the safer choice.
- **No hides.** A guinea pig without a place to hide is a stressed guinea pig. Always provide at least one hide per pig.
- **Only one water source for multiple pigs.** Dominant guinea pigs can guard a single water bottle. With two or more pigs, provide at least two water sources so everyone can drink.

## Start With the Right Foundation

A proper cage setup is the single best thing you can do for your guinea pig's long-term health and happiness. Get the size right, choose safe bedding, provide the essentials, and place it somewhere your pig can be part of the family.
