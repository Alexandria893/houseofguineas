Adopting a guinea pig changed my life in ways I never expected. When I brought home Milo — my first guinea pig — I thought I was getting a quiet, low-maintenance pet. What I got instead was a tiny, opinionated companion who wheeks at the sound of the refrigerator door and has strong feelings about which vegetables are acceptable. Four guinea pigs later (Milo, Luca, Coco, and Kai), I can tell you that these animals give back so much more than they ask for. As an [exotic veterinary assistant](/san-francisco-california-exotic-veterinary-experience) in San Francisco, I've also seen firsthand what happens when people adopt without doing their homework. This guide covers everything you need to know before bringing a guinea pig home.

## Why Adopt a Guinea Pig?

Guinea pigs are one of the most social, affectionate small animals you can own. They recognize their owners, vocalize to communicate (the famous "wheek!" when they hear a bag crinkle is impossible not to love), and genuinely bond with the people who care for them. With a lifespan of 5 to 7 years, they're a meaningful commitment — but not the 10-to-15-year commitment of a dog or cat.

They're excellent pets for families with children, apartment dwellers, and anyone who wants an interactive companion without the demands of a larger animal. Guinea pigs don't need walks and won't destroy your furniture. What they will do is learn your schedule, greet you at the cage door, and make you feel genuinely loved by an animal that weighs two pounds.

One thing I feel strongly about: **always adopt from rescues, not pet stores.** Pet store guinea pigs often come from breeding mills with poor conditions, and buying from stores fuels that cycle. Rescues are full of healthy guinea pigs who need homes — many of them already socialized and litter-trained.

## Where to Adopt Guinea Pigs

If you're in the San Francisco Bay Area, you have excellent options. The SF SPCA occasionally has guinea pigs available, and there are dedicated small animal rescue organizations throughout the Bay Area that specialize in rehoming guinea pigs and other small mammals. Search for guinea pig rescues near you — many post their available animals online.

Beyond local rescues, [Petfinder.com](https://www.petfinder.com/) is a great resource for finding adoptable guinea pigs in your area. You may also find guinea pigs on Craigslist from owners who can no longer care for them — just exercise caution and ask about the pig's health history.

**When choosing your guinea pig, look for:**
* Alert, bright eyes with no discharge
* A clean, smooth coat with no bald patches
* Active, curious behavior — a healthy pig should be moving around
* Clear breathing with no wheezing or clicking sounds

## The Real Cost of Guinea Pig Ownership

Let's talk numbers, because guinea pigs are not as cheap as people assume.

* **Adoption fee:** $25-75 from a rescue
* **Cage:** $80-200 for a proper C&C (cubes and coroplast) cage — the minimum recommended size is 7.5 square feet for a pair
* **Bedding:** $315 one-time investment for [Clinical Series fleece liners](/clinical-series-guinea-pig-fleece-cage-liners), which last years — versus $240-480 per year for disposable paper or wood bedding
* **Hay, pellets, and fresh vegetables:** $40-60 per month
* **Exotic vet visits:** $50-100 per wellness checkup — and you must find an [exotic vet](/san-francisco-bay-area-exotic-veterinarians), not a regular dog-and-cat vet

**Estimated first-year total: $600-1,000** for a pair of guinea pigs, depending on your choices. Fleece liners pay for themselves within the first year and save money every year after that.

## Your Guinea Pig Supply Checklist

Get everything set up before you bring your guinea pigs home. Scrambling for supplies on day one adds stress — for you and for them.

* A C&C cage or suitable enclosure (see our [small mammal resources page](/small-mammal-resources) for the Critter Colony and other options)
* [Fleece cage liners](/clinical-series-guinea-pig-fleece-cage-liners) or other safe bedding
* Timothy hay — unlimited supply (this is 80% of their diet)
* Guinea pig pellets fortified with vitamin C (guinea pigs cannot produce their own)
* A water bottle and a sturdy food bowl
* At least 2 hides — guinea pigs need places to feel safe
* A hay rack to keep hay clean and accessible
* Nail clippers designed for small animals

## The First Week: What to Expect

Here's the truth: your new guinea pigs will be terrified. That's completely normal. They're prey animals in a brand new environment with unfamiliar sights, sounds, and smells. Don't take it personally when they run and hide every time you walk by.

**Days 1-3:** Let them settle in. Keep the cage in a calm area away from loud TVs or high-traffic zones. Don't try to pick them up yet — just make sure they have fresh hay, water, and vegetables.

**Days 3-5:** Sit near the cage and talk softly. Offer small pieces of lettuce or bell pepper through the cage bars so they start associating you with good things.

**Days 5-7:** Try gentle hand-feeding inside the cage. If they approach you, let them sniff your hand. Don't grab from above — that mimics a predator.

And don't be alarmed if you see your guinea pig suddenly leap into the air, twist, and kick — that's called "popcorning," and it means they're happy. It's one of the most joyful things you'll ever witness.

## Guinea Pigs Need a Friend

This is non-negotiable: **guinea pigs should always be kept in pairs or small groups.** They are herd animals, and a solo guinea pig can become depressed, anxious, and even physically ill from loneliness. In Switzerland, it's actually illegal to keep a single guinea pig.

Adopt a same-sex pair, or have one pig neutered if you're pairing a male and female. If you're introducing a new guinea pig to an existing one, do it on neutral territory — a floor pen or bathtub works well. Supervise the introduction closely, expect some rumbling and mounting (that's normal dominance behavior), and only separate them if there's actual biting that draws blood.

## Health Essentials for New Owners

The single most important thing you can do for your guinea pig's health is **find an exotic vet before you need one.** Don't wait until there's an emergency to discover that your regular vet doesn't treat guinea pigs. We maintain a list of [Bay Area exotic veterinarians](/san-francisco-bay-area-exotic-veterinarians) to help you get started.

**Other health essentials:**
* **Weekly weight checks** — buy a simple kitchen scale and weigh each pig at the same time every week. A drop of more than 2 ounces in a week is a red flag.
* **Learn the warning signs** — know the [5 signs your guinea pig needs a vet](/post/signs-your-guinea-pig-needs-a-vet) so you can act fast when something is off.
* **Know what an emergency looks like** — read our guide on [exotic pet emergency signs](/post/exotic-pet-emergency-signs) so you're prepared for the unexpected.
