Reptiles and amphibians have very specific environmental, dietary, and handling needs that differ dramatically from traditional pets and even from each other. As an exotic veterinary assistant, I've provided hands-on care for bearded dragons, snakes, and turtles — and through my clinical training at [Bay Area Bird and Exotics Hospital](https://www.birdandexoticsvet.com), I've gained working knowledge of geckos, axolotls, frogs, and other species as well.

Below you'll find species-specific care essentials that inform my [in-home care](/home/services/exotic-pet-care-services-in-home) and [boarding](/home/services/exotic-pet-care-services-boarding) services here in San Francisco. Whether you're a first-time reptile owner or an experienced keeper, understanding these fundamentals is key to keeping your pet healthy and thriving.

## Reptile Care

<details class="care-details" open>
  <summary class="care-summary">Bearded Dragon Care</summary>
  <div class="care-content">
    <p>Bearded dragons are one of the most popular pet reptiles — and for good reason. They're personable, relatively hardy, and can form real bonds with their owners. But they require precise environmental management to stay healthy:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Temperature Gradients:</strong> A proper basking spot of 100-110&deg;F on the warm side and 80-85&deg;F on the cool side. Nighttime temps should not drop below 70&deg;F. I monitor these carefully with digital thermometers during every care visit.</li>
      <li><strong>UVB Lighting:</strong> Full-spectrum UVB is essential for calcium metabolism. Without adequate UVB exposure, bearded dragons are at serious risk for Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD) — one of the most common and preventable health issues I observed as a veterinary assistant.</li>
      <li><strong>Diet:</strong> Juveniles need 70-80% insects (dusted with calcium and vitamin D3 supplements) and 20-30% fresh greens. Adults shift to roughly 80% greens and 20% insects. I prepare species-appropriate meals and monitor appetite closely.</li>
      <li><strong>Signs to Watch:</strong> Lethargy, black beard stress marks, sunken eyes, rubbery jaw (MBD indicator), loss of appetite, and irregular shedding.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</details>

<details class="care-details">
  <summary class="care-summary">Gecko Care <em>(Leopard, Crested, Fat-Tailed)</em></summary>
  <div class="care-content">
    <p>Geckos are diverse — a leopard gecko's needs differ significantly from a crested gecko's. Through my veterinary training, I've learned the key differences in their care:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Leopard Geckos:</strong> Require belly heat (under-tank heater) with a warm side of 88-92&deg;F and a cool side around 75&deg;F. Low humidity (30-40%). Insectivores — crickets, mealworms, and dubia roaches dusted with supplements.</li>
      <li><strong>Crested Geckos:</strong> Thrive at room temperature (72-78&deg;F) with moderate humidity (60-80%). Misting 1-2 times daily is essential. Primarily eat prepared crested gecko diet (CGD) with occasional insects as treats.</li>
      <li><strong>Shedding:</strong> Incomplete sheds are a common issue, especially on toes and tail tips. Retained shed should be checked regularly, as constricted circulation from stuck shed can cause permanent damage.</li>
      <li><strong>Signs to Watch:</strong> Dropped tail (stress response), stuck shed, sunken eyes, weight loss, and refusal to eat.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</details>

<details class="care-details">
  <summary class="care-summary">Snake Care <em>(Ball Pythons, Corn Snakes, King Snakes)</em></summary>
  <div class="care-content">
    <p>Snakes are low-maintenance compared to some reptiles, but their environmental needs are non-negotiable:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Thermoregulation:</strong> Snakes are ectotherms and depend entirely on their enclosure for temperature regulation. A warm side of 88-92&deg;F and a cool side of 75-80&deg;F allows them to self-regulate. I verify temperatures at every visit.</li>
      <li><strong>Humidity:</strong> Ball pythons need 60-70% humidity (higher during shedding). Corn and king snakes need 40-60%. Improper humidity leads to respiratory infections and stuck sheds.</li>
      <li><strong>Feeding Schedule:</strong> Juveniles eat every 5-7 days; adults every 10-14 days depending on species and size. I follow your established feeding schedule and monitor for regurgitation or refusal.</li>
      <li><strong>Handling:</strong> Snakes need 48 hours after feeding before handling. I respect your snake's comfort level and watch for defensive postures (S-coil, hissing, tail rattling).</li>
      <li><strong>Signs to Watch:</strong> Wheezing or bubbling (respiratory infection), retained eye caps after shedding, scale rot, stargazing, and prolonged food refusal.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</details>

<details class="care-details">
  <summary class="care-summary">Turtle & Tortoise Care</summary>
  <div class="care-content">
    <p>Turtles and tortoises are long-lived pets with very different needs depending on whether they're aquatic, semi-aquatic, or terrestrial:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Aquatic Turtles:</strong> Need clean, filtered water (partial water changes weekly), a basking platform with UVB lighting at 85-90&deg;F, and water temperature of 75-80&deg;F. Shell health depends on both water quality and UV exposure.</li>
      <li><strong>Tortoises:</strong> Require appropriate substrate for burrowing, a warm basking area (90-95&deg;F), and a cooler retreat. UVB is essential for shell and bone health. Diet varies by species — grasses and hay for grazers (sulcatas), leafy greens for others.</li>
      <li><strong>Shell Health:</strong> I check for soft spots, discoloration, and pyramiding (raised scutes from improper diet or humidity). Shell problems often develop slowly, so regular observation is critical.</li>
      <li><strong>Signs to Watch:</strong> Soft shell, swollen eyes (vitamin A deficiency), runny nose, lethargy, and loss of appetite.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</details>

## Amphibian Care

<details class="care-details">
  <summary class="care-summary">Axolotl Care</summary>
  <div class="care-content">
    <p>Axolotls are fully aquatic salamanders that are sensitive to water quality and temperature. From my clinical training, here are the essentials for keeping them healthy:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Water Temperature:</strong> Must stay between 60-68&deg;F — axolotls are cold-water animals. Temperatures above 72&deg;F cause dangerous stress and can suppress their immune system, which is especially important to monitor during San Francisco's warmer months.</li>
      <li><strong>Water Quality:</strong> Ammonia and nitrites must be at 0 ppm; nitrates below 20 ppm. Regular partial water changes (20% weekly) with dechlorinated water are essential.</li>
      <li><strong>Diet:</strong> Primarily earthworms (nightcrawlers) — the most nutritious staple. Can also eat bloodworms and specially formulated pellets. Feed 2-3 times per week for adults.</li>
      <li><strong>Substrate:</strong> Fine sand or bare bottom only. Gravel is a serious impaction risk — axolotls vacuum-feed and will swallow small stones.</li>
      <li><strong>Signs to Watch:</strong> Curled gill filaments (stress or poor water quality), floating, loss of appetite, fungal patches, and gill deterioration.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</details>

<details class="care-details">
  <summary class="care-summary">Frog & Newt Care <em>(Tree Frogs, Pacman Frogs, Fire-Bellied Newts)</em></summary>
  <div class="care-content">
    <p>Frogs and newts have permeable skin that makes them exceptionally sensitive to environmental conditions. From my veterinary training, here are the care fundamentals:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Humidity:</strong> Most species need 60-80% humidity maintained through misting, live plants, and proper ventilation. Monitoring humidity with a hygrometer and misting enclosures regularly is essential.</li>
      <li><strong>Temperature:</strong> Varies by species — tropical frogs (tree frogs, pacman frogs) prefer 75-82&deg;F, while temperate newts thrive at 65-72&deg;F. Overheating is a common and dangerous mistake.</li>
      <li><strong>Water Quality:</strong> All water must be dechlorinated — chlorine and chloramine are toxic to amphibians. Semi-aquatic species need clean, filtered water with regular partial changes.</li>
      <li><strong>Diet:</strong> Primarily live insects (crickets, fruit flies for small species, earthworms for larger ones), dusted with calcium supplements. Pacman frogs can also eat appropriately sized mice as they grow.</li>
      <li><strong>Handling:</strong> Should be minimized. Amphibians absorb substances through their skin, so hands must be clean, wet, and free of soap residue, lotions, or hand sanitizer.</li>
      <li><strong>Signs to Watch:</strong> Skin lesions or redness, bloating (edema), lethargy, refusal to eat, and abnormal posture.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</details>
